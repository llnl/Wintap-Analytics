# Windows Attribution Validation — Historical Executive Summary

**Scope and evidence status.** This summary preserves the technical findings from a complete review of two developer-documentation archives: **46 files comprising 42 Markdown documents and four empty placeholders**. They contained architecture decisions, engineering notes, and summarized validation results—not raw telemetry datasets. At the Architect's request, source references have been omitted because the original archives are intended for deletion. This is a historical account of reported results, not independently reproduced evidence or certification of the current implementation. Confidence is limited by the absence of retained source references; the review date above does not imply the tests were rerun. Machine-specific identifiers and observed software inventories are intentionally omitted.

**Disclosure assessment.** The reviewed material contained absolute development/scratch paths, observed application and process names, executable-to-file associations, filesystem/volume details, process identifiers and hashes, and precise operational timestamps. Together, these could reveal workstation software inventory and activity without naming a machine explicitly. The review found no explicit hostnames, IP addresses, actual account names, credentials, or secrets in the two archives; this finding is not a security certification. Referenced logs and artifacts outside the archives were not included. The appropriate retained record is this sanitized technical synthesis rather than a reproduction of the environment-specific observations.

**Historical architecture and foundational testing.** The earlier archive records the decision that core—not individual sensors—owns process identity and parent linkage; sensors supply event-time attributes, Stop events recover missing information from durable storage, and DuckDB is the initial persistence and lookup mechanism. It also specifies per-platform test projects, starting with a shared-API constructor test. Its concrete implementation evidence is a successful Release build and **9/9 tests passing** for an ETW UserSID extraction helper, covering normal extraction, null markers, and malformed payloads. It contains no performance measurements or comprehensive live-validation results. That archive was already marked retired, with migration to the shared Analytics wiki reported but not independently verified during this review.

**Attribution measurement and corrective work.** Most of the later archive documents an October investigation into unresolved File and Registry process ownership. Attribution failure rates must use matching health-check event counts—not downstream Parquet row counts, because serialization aggregates events. The initial analysis reported **9.84 million unresolved events out of 66.74 million checked: 14.75% overall, 8.06% File, and 43.41% Registry**. The recorded acceptance target is **below 5% for each event family over a fresh-start 24-hour run**, including startup failures. Documented work addresses diagnostics, sub-millisecond timestamp comparisons, cache indexing, stable process identities, Windows reconciliation behavior, and shared owner-resolution routing; approval and implementation evidence vary by item. Reported automated results include **39 focused/431 full-suite passes** for diagnostics and **13 focused/439 full-suite passes** for timestamp-query corrections. These are recorded audit results, not tests rerun during this review.

**Validation approach and what it proves.** The documents combine deterministic lifecycle/PID-reuse tests with periodic held-child and early-exit file workloads, plus **20 sampled File Write events approximately every five minutes**. This provides useful coverage, but plausible attribution is distinct from independently verified ownership and successful persistence. Early probes were compromised by suppressed logging, a command-wrapper failure, and background activity that invalidated exclusive-owner assumptions. A reboot investigation found **257 process-hash repairs**, with all **51 missing identities among 80 samples** matching previously repaired keys—strong evidence that changing an established identity can break downstream linkage. Subsequent runs showed zero repairs and improved sampled linkage. Finalized-Parquet verification remained a separate follow-up rather than a demonstrated end-to-end guarantee.

**Strong extended-run evidence—but not a clean success.** One **18-hour-48-minute** cohort reconciled **1,120 reporting windows**, with File unresolved attribution at **0.000367%** and Registry at **40.114%**. Its probes reported **218 PASS, eight INCONCLUSIVE, and zero FAIL**, with **4,480 samples** and no sampled missing identities; nevertheless, Registry quality remained poor, alongside **179 Esper timeouts, 289 capture-loss reports, and 140 failed recoveries**. A later, separate **87-minute** deployment looked encouraging—File **0.000129%**, Registry **4.971623%**, and **17 PASS/one INCONCLUSIVE**—but most Registry failures occurred at startup and the run was too short to satisfy the acceptance gate. These were different cohorts, not a controlled before/after benchmark.

**Latest recorded outcome and remaining risks.** The latest reviewed record, dated October 14, covers **12 hours 9 minutes and 729 reporting windows**: File unresolved attribution was **0.184646%**, Registry **13.898192%**, and the final Registry interval reached **78.39%**. The validator produced **three PASS followed by 32 INCONCLUSIVE**, then terminated after a cleanup exception, leaving roughly nine hours without probe/sample coverage. The run also recorded **49 Esper timeouts, 156 capture losses, and 77 failed recoveries**. Conversely, 146 maintenance sweeps continued without live-identity repairs, with a maximum duration of **279 ms**. The defensible historical conclusion is that **File unresolved-attribution rates improved substantially, but sustained Registry attribution and validator reliability remained unresolved**; neither low File failure rates nor passing synthetic probes establish complete telemetry correctness.

**Other durable design material.** The remaining documents explain why established process identities should remain stable, why PID reuse requires distinct identities, and why event timestamps cannot automatically substitute for process creation times. They also describe an approved—but not verified as implemented in the reviewed collection—Windows file-path normalization change: normalize before grouping/hashing, prefer valid drive/UNC mappings, preserve native-path fallbacks and distinct snapshot identities, and refresh mappings outside the event hot path. Important tradeoffs remain: lowercasing can lose case-sensitive distinctions, current mappings cannot reconstruct historical topology, and path changes affect grouping and hashes. TraceProcessor comparison, MemoryMap identity checks, and persisted-output validation remained investigations or future work.

**Retention and interpretation.** Preserve the architectural rationale, regression descriptions, test scope and pass counts, cohort durations and denominators, failure rates, capture/timeout counts, and explicit limitations and unfinished gates. Any necessary original evidence should remain only in an appropriately controlled location; this summary does not establish that such an archive exists. Future evidence records should replace observed process names, paths, identifiers, and exact activity timestamps with neutral labels or relative timing while retaining relationships needed to understand defects. Repetitive planning exchanges, duplicated logs, retired scaffolding, and machine-specific troubleshooting offer less lasting value. Most importantly, retain negative and inconclusive results: they prevent encouraging short tests from being mistaken for completed validation.
